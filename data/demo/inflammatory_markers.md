# Inflammatory Markers — Clinical Interpretation

Title: Inflammatory Markers Guide
Document Type: educational

## C-Reactive Protein (CRP)

### Overview
CRP is an acute-phase reactant produced by the liver in response to inflammation. It is a non-specific marker that rises rapidly (within 6-8 hours) in response to infection, tissue injury, or inflammation.

### Normal Values
- Normal: < 10 mg/L
- Mild elevation: 10–40 mg/L
- Moderate elevation: 40–100 mg/L
- Severe elevation: > 100 mg/L

### Clinical Interpretation

Elevated CRP may indicate:
- Bacterial infection (often > 50 mg/L, sometimes > 200 mg/L)
- Viral infection (usually mild elevation, < 50 mg/L)
- Autoimmune diseases (rheumatoid arthritis, lupus)
- Tissue necrosis or injury
- Malignancy
- Post-surgical inflammation

CRP is useful for:
- Distinguishing bacterial from viral infection (higher in bacterial)
- Monitoring treatment response (decreases with effective therapy)
- Assessing disease severity in infections

### Limitations
- Non-specific (elevated in many conditions)
- Can be normal in early infection
- Affected by liver disease
- Not useful alone for diagnosis

## Procalcitonin

### Overview
Procalcitonin is a more specific marker for bacterial infection compared to CRP. It rises within 2-4 hours of bacterial infection and peaks at 24 hours.

### Normal Values
- Normal: < 0.1 ng/mL
- Low risk of bacterial infection: < 0.25 ng/mL
- Possible bacterial infection: 0.25–0.5 ng/mL
- Likely bacterial infection: > 0.5 ng/mL
- Severe bacterial infection/sepsis: > 2 ng/mL

### Clinical Use
- Helps differentiate bacterial from viral respiratory infections
- Guides antibiotic initiation and duration
- Prognostic marker in sepsis

## Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR)

### Overview
ESR measures the rate at which red blood cells settle in a tube. It is an older, less specific inflammatory marker that changes more slowly than CRP.

### Normal Values
- Men: 0–15 mm/hr (age < 50), 0–20 mm/hr (age > 50)
- Women: 0–20 mm/hr (age < 50), 0–30 mm/hr (age > 50)

### Elevated ESR
Causes include:
- Infection
- Autoimmune diseases (especially temporal arteritis, polymyalgia rheumatica)
- Malignancy (especially multiple myeloma)
- Anemia
- Pregnancy
- Advanced age
